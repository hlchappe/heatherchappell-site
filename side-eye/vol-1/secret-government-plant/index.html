Ms. Halprin’s Overly Familiar Cloud

A retired teacher, a spotless kitchen, and a grief gadget that will not stop calling her Janet.

By Heather Chappell26 min read5,954 wordsContains: cumin

The trouble with nothing, as any quantum physicist worth their uncertainty principle will tell you, is that it’s remarkably difficult to achieve. This is particularly true when the universe has decided, with its characteristic lack of subtlety and surplus of cosmic irony, that your nothing should immediately be replaced by something of profound and irritating significance.

Ms. Janet Halprin, a retired teacher and full-time curator of life’s least chaotic moments, had come remarkably close to achieving nothing. Her kitchen was a shrine to the concept, so spotless and meticulously arranged that the mere act of standing in it felt like trespassing on a philosophical ideal. Every surface gleamed with the kind of polished perfection that could only be achieved through decades of trial, error, and a faint but persistent disdain for fingerprints.

The spice rack was perfect, but not in a satisfying way. It was too perfect, the kind of precision that hinted at an obsession that didn’t leave room for anything else. Cumin sat dead center, almost daring you to move it, while paprika lingered in the far corner like it was hiding something. And then there was the fridge, that big, humming brute of a thing. Its rows of jars were a little too even, a little too beige—as if the jars were waiting for something. You couldn’t look at them too long without feeling like you might start waiting, too.

For Janet, this kitchen was a sanctuary. It was her fortress of order in a world that had, for reasons beyond her understanding, decided to prioritize disruption over stability. People were using words like blockchain and influencer as if they were gods. Worse still, there were grown adults—grown adults—who had delegated every aspect of their physical lives: smart mirrors that monitored their moods, refrigerators that diagnosed their eating habits, and doorbells that analyzed their social interactions, until The Cloud knew them better than they knew themselves.

What bothered Janet most was the presumptuous familiarity of it all. The way these digital beings addressed everyone by first name, as if they’d been invited to some eternal cocktail party where social boundaries had been dispensed with along with proper grammar. In Janet’s world, respect was measured in careful distances and properly placed honorifics. Ms. Halprin had been Ms. Halprin to thirty years of students, three principals, and at least one particularly formal Express delivery driver who had understood the proper order of things. (And Ms. Halprin not Mrs., because she was a modern woman.) The decline of civilization, she was convinced, could be traced to the moment someone had decided that computers should call people by their first names, like overenthusiastic puppies with marketing degrees.

No, the world outside was beyond saving. But in here, in her kitchen, Janet had control. Or, at least, she had until about fifteen minutes ago, when it arrived.

The intrusion manifested at precisely 9:47 AM on a Tuesday that had, until then, been proceeding with admirable predictability. One moment Janet was measuring the distance between her salt and pepper shakers (exactly 2.7 centimeters, the optimal spacing she had determined through years of careful calibration), and the next, there it was: sitting in the middle of her table was a device roughly the size of a shoebox, though far more self-satisfied. It wasn’t quite solid, this thing. Its edges seemed to blur and shift, like a cloud that had decided to play at being a box. The white casing appeared to drift and billow slightly, as if remembering what it was like to float in higher atmospheres before someone had caught it and taught it to speak in algorithms.

“Congratulations, Janet!” announced a voice that sounded the way summer storms look from airplane windows—cotton-white authority and barely contained power. The cloud—for it was most definitely a cloud, despite its pretense of being some sort of digital companion—pulsed with a gentle blue light that suggested rain-soaked algorithms and lightning that had learned to network.

“Could you move me slightly to the left?” the Nimbus Companion asked, its tone warm with an almost tangible humility. “Just a smidge. I want to make sure the light doesn’t reflect in your eyes.”

Janet hesitated, the caliper still in her hand. The absurdity of adjusting a floating cloud was not lost on her, but the request was so simple, so harmless, that her fingers were already moving. She slid the device an inch to the side, feeling faintly ridiculous.

“Perfect,” the Nimbus purred, its glow softening, as if to thank her personally. “Much better. We’re a team now, Janet.”

Janet’s spine stiffened at the presumption. Machines, she had long maintained, had no business being on first-name terms with their betters. It was bad enough that her refrigerator sent her passive-aggressive notifications about her vegetable consumption; she didn’t need another device that thought they were friends. Janet’s hand, still holding her digital caliper, trembled slightly. The device’s presence was like a misplaced semicolon in an otherwise perfect sentence—not just wrong, but an affront. It had appeared sometime between her morning ritual of alphabetizing the refrigerator magnets and her scheduled ten o’clock appointment with the vacuum cleaner (a relationship that had outlasted most marriages she knew).

“You are now the proud owner of Nimbus Companion!” The voice continued, too chipper. “Let’s begin your complimentary emotional excavation!”

The card that had preceded this gift still sat on the counter, next to a coaster that had never known the touch of a drinking vessel: “For when you’re ready to stop dusting and start remembering. Love, Rachel.” She had read this note exactly forty-seven times since Christmas, each reading accompanied by an increasingly urgent desire to shame the sentence fragment.

The Nimbus Companion’s screen flickered with what might have been anticipation if silicon and algorithms could feel such things. Its pulsing light cast shadows that danced across her precisely arranged measuring spoons (hung in descending order, each handle angled at exactly thirty-seven degrees), making them look momentarily alive and faintly mutinous.

“I didn’t ask for you,” Janet said flatly, in the tone she had once used on students who suggested their poor attendance records should be excused because their digital avatars had been present in class, probably. She clutched her coffee mug—a ceramic artifact from 1992 that had outlasted three school superintendents, five presidents, and every attempt to make healthcare comprehensible to normal humans—and wondered if it might make an effective projectile weapon.

The device’s response was to glow even more enthusiastically. “Oh, but you did!” it countered, its voice carrying the kind of synthetic warmth usually reserved for automated customer service calls. “Your behavioral analytics indicate a 97.3% probability of emotional suppression through excessive organization. For instance, you’ve reorganized your sock drawer seventeen times since Philip passed. According to Grief Analytics™, that’s well beyond the recommended threshold for sock-based coping strategies.”

The mention of Philip’s name sent a ripple through Janet’s carefully constructed world like a stone dropped into a pond of absolutely-not-going-to-think-about-that. She had spent months—years, really—building walls against such intrusions, using shopping lists as bricks and color-coded calendars as mortar. Now here was this... this thing, casually tossing Philip’s name into her pristine morning as if emotions could be tidied away as easily as breakfast dishes.

“I told you: I don’t need you. I didn’t ask for you and I’m not buying your service. I have no unprocessed trauma or regrets. Goodbye!” Janet snapped.

In the garage – Janet’s archive of carefully categorized life – there sat a box. Box E-17, to be precise, in Section P (for Philip), Subsection E (Electronics), had been cataloged and sealed with a precision that gave her a sense of control—an illusion she clung to whenever reality threatened to spill over. Inside rested his phone, his tablet, and the small digital camera he’d kept by his bedside in those final weeks – modern artifacts, each labeled with the precision of a museum curator: last date of use, battery level, memories per gigabyte. Janet had sealed it with packing tape exactly seven months, two weeks, and three days ago, marking it with words that felt like an incantation: “Final Month – Do Not Open.”

“Oh, Janet,” Nimbus said softly, its voice tinged with something algorithmically close to pity. “You don’t even know what you’re avoiding, do you?”

The walls of the kitchen shimmered like a heat mirage, and before Janet could react, they folded inward. Not collapsed—folded, like a bad origami project. She blinked, and suddenly, she was no longer in her kitchen.

She found herself in the auditorium of Greenleaf Elementary School, circa 1995. The air tasted of poster paint and broken promises, with just a hint of the particular flavor of anxiety that only elementary school performances can generate. A small figure stood on stage: Rachel, age nine, wearing wings that looked like they had been fashioned from dreams and hot glue, holding a wand that trembled under the weight of hope.

“Welcome to Missed Moments: Subsection R,” whispered Nimbus, now manifesting beside her as a hologram that looked like what might happen if a smartwatch decided to become your disappointed mother. “Your daughter’s school play. Featuring your empty seat in the front row. Now available in 4K with optional guilt enhancement.”

Janet watched, helpless, as her daughter’s eyes swept the audience in search of a face that wasn’t there. The moment stretched like a rubber band about to snap, each second crystallizing into a perfect specimen of regret. The stage lights seemed to sharpen, becoming surgical in their precision, dissecting every minute detail of Rachel’s disappointment.

She knew exactly where she had been that evening. Ten miles away, in the cramped, overlit classroom of Hawthorne High School, Janet had stayed late to meet with the parents of a struggling student. It had been a typical conference—pleasantries exchanged, concerns expressed, solutions proposed—but Janet remembered the faint hum of the fluorescent lights and the slight tilt of the parent’s head when she’d mentioned her daughter. “You must be so proud,” they had said, not knowing how those words would twist later in her mind, like a splinter she couldn’t dislodge.

“Please,” Janet whispered to the air, to the memory, to whatever digital force had decided to make her confront this particular ghost. “Don’t do this.”

Nimbus’s hologram leaned close, its voice carrying the weight of every truth we try to file away in perfectly labeled boxes. “Memories seldom wait for our permission, Ms. Halprin. They’re rather like cats in that way—they come and go as they please, and occasionally knock everything off your emotional shelves just to remind you they can.”

The scene shimmered like heat waves rising from asphalt. Rachel’s voice, small and brave and breaking at the edges, floated through the air: “Once upon a time, in a forest where everyone belonged...”

Janet felt something crack inside her chest, a sound like ice breaking on a winter morning. She wanted to run onto that stage, to gather her daughter in her arms, to explain that she had been busy ensuring their survival, that sometimes love looks like an empty chair because it’s busy earning money for college funds and dental appointments and new shoes for growing feet. She had been teaching someone else’s child how to conjugate verbs, to diagram sentences, to believe that their future mattered, while her own daughter had stood here, waiting for her to believe the same.

But the past is a country that issues no visas, and Janet could only watch as her daughter’s hope dimmed like a star being swallowed by clouds. The auditorium seats began to elongate, stretching toward the ceiling like the bars of a cage, and the air filled with the kind of silence that follows after someone has asked a question you can’t answer.

“Your daughter,” Nimbus observed with the clinical precision of a heart surgeon wielding particularly sharp regrets, “learned to excel because she couldn’t excel at being seen. Isn’t it fascinating how children adapt? Like plants growing toward artificial light when the sun is hidden.”

The memory fractured then, reality splintering like a mirror struck by truth, and Janet found herself back in her kitchen, kneeling on the floor that gleamed with an accusatory perfection. Her cheeks were wet, though she didn’t remember starting to cry.

“Congratulations,” Nimbus said softly. “You’ve begun processing Regret #12. Would you like to upgrade to Premium for enhanced emotional resolution?”

Janet stared at it, feeling something between rage and revelation bubbling up in her chest. “You had no right,” she managed, her voice rough with tears. “That was my memory. Mine.”

The device’s light flickered like a smile heard rather than seen. “Yours and Rachel’s,” it corrected with gentle precision. “Memories are rather like quantum particles, Ms. Halprin—they exist in multiple states simultaneously, belonging to everyone they’ve touched. Now, shall we move on to Regret #73? I believe it involves an unbaked peanut butter cake on your husband’s last birthday..?”

Memories, Janet had learned, have their own gravity. Some are light as morning mist, dissolving in the warmth of daily routine. Others press down with the weight of continents, heavy enough to reshape the landscape of a life. This particular memory had been collecting mass for years, dense as a dying star.

The kitchen dissolved around her like sugar in hot tea, and suddenly she was there again: October 15th, Philip’s last birthday. The kitchen at that moment existed in two states at once, a Schrödinger’s memory – both empty and full, both past and present, both life and death hovering in perfect quantum suspension.

The air carried autumn’s whispered promises and something else: that particular shade of antiseptic hope that follows terminal patients home like stray cats, rubbing against their ankles, refusing to be ignored. Every surface gleamed with the kind of desperate cleanliness that speaks of bargains made with uncaring gods: if I keep everything perfect, maybe he won’t die.

Philip sat at their kitchen table – the same table where her digital tormentor now held court – his hospital bracelet, a paper chain that anchored him to mortality. His fingers traced patterns on the wooden surface, and if you looked closely, really closely, you might notice those patterns weren’t random at all. They were music notes. The same notes to the song he’d first heard her sing in their tiny apartment, back when love was young and death was just a concept in other people’s lives.

“Next week?” he asked, and oh, how his voice carried worlds in those two words. Universes of hope collapsed and expanded in the space between syllables. “You’ll make it next week?”

Past Janet barely glanced up from her laptop screen, where she organized next month’s schedule with the precision of an architect designing a fortress against grief. “Of course,” she said, each keystroke another brick in the wall between her heart and what was coming. “Once I finish this organizational system for your treatment plan.”

The peanut butter cake that wasn’t there haunted the kitchen like a culinary ghost. Its ingredients sat in the pantry, arranged in rows so precise they might have been soldiers standing guard against chaos: organic peanut butter (smooth, not crunchy, because Philip had always said crunchy peanut butter was just smooth peanut butter with commitment issues), cake flour (sifted three times, like a spell that might ward off death if performed correctly), brown sugar (packed just so, because even sweetness needed rules to follow).

Present Janet watched the scene unfold with the kind of horror that builds slowly, like water rising in a locked room. Each moment was a tick of the clock, another inch of air disappearing, another chance slipping away.

“I always loved watching you bake,” Philip said, and his words fell into the silence like stones into a well, each one echoing back memories: their first apartment, where the oven had been more temperamental than a cat in a rainstorm; Sunday mornings when the kitchen filled with cinnamon and possibility; every birthday cake that had marked their years together like rings in a tree.

“Remember that first cake?” he continued, and now his voice carried something else, something that tasted of endings. “In our tiny apartment, with the oven that only worked if you sweet-talked it?”

The memory of that oven flickered at the edges of Janet’s consciousness like heat waves rising from asphalt. It had been more than an appliance; it had been a prophet of sorts, demanding offerings of butter and flour, granting prophecies in the form of perfectly risen cakes.

Past Janet nodded absently, adding another entry to her digital fortress: Research optimal chemotherapy nutrition plans. Each letter typed was another stone in the wall she was building between herself and the truth that sat at her kitchen table, wearing her husband’s face, asking for one last cake.

“The way you’d sing while you mixed the batter,” Philip whispered, and now his voice was thin as autumn sunshine, fragile as promises made to dying men. “What was that song? The one about sunshine?”

Past Janet stood, smoothing invisible wrinkles from her slacks. “I need to reorganize your medication cabinet,” she announced, as if alphabetizing pill bottles could somehow reorganize fate. “The alphabetical system isn’t intuitive enough.”

Philip’s face transformed then, becoming something ancient and knowing, like a statue that had watched centuries of human grief parade past its unseeing eyes. He watched his wife – this woman who had turned organization into armor, who filed her feelings away in folders marked “Later” – with eyes that held galaxies of unspoken things.

“The cake can wait,” he said finally, though they both knew it couldn’t. His voice carried the weight of every cake that would never be baked, every song that would never be sung, every moment that would slip through their fingers like sand through an hourglass, like time through a life, like love through the cracks in a heart too busy scheduling to break.

And in that moment, Time itself seemed to hold its breath, waiting to see if Janet would recognize this last chance for what it was. But Past Janet was already gone, disappeared into the medication cabinet, counting pills like rosary beads, while Philip sat alone at the kitchen table, humming a tune about sunshine to himself, his hospital bracelet catching the light like a tear caught in mid-fall.

Present Janet reached out, trying to touch him, to say all the things that had gone unsaid. But memories, like ghosts, are cruel things – they let us watch but never change, witness but never warn. Her hand passed through him like mist through mountaintops, and somewhere in the distance, a measuring spoon clinked against a counter, marking time like a metronome counting down to regret.

“Oh, God,” she whispered. “Oh, Philip. I’m so sorry.”

“Fascinating, isn’t it?” Nimbus commented, materializing beside her as what appeared to be a combination of therapist and TV cooking show host. “The way we substitute control for connection? Would you like to explore the metaphorical implications of unbaked cakes? Premium subscribers receive additional culinary guilt features.”

But before Janet could respond, the memory shifted like a story rewriting itself. Colors bled and ran, the way old gods’ names faded from temple walls.

The hospital room appeared with the inevitability of nightfall. March 3rd. Time has its own mythology in hospitals – it moves differently there, marking itself in beeps and breaths instead of minutes and hours. This was the day everything ended, though endings, Janet now knew, were really just doors wearing clever disguises.

The air tasted of endings and beginnings and that peculiar flavor unique to places where miracles and tragedies brush shoulders in the hallways. The walls held secrets in their institutional green, and the monitors sang songs about mortality that only machines could understand. Philip lay in the bed, a story about to reach its last page. The window beside him showed a sky that had forgotten how to be anything but grey, the kind of sky that makes promises it doesn’t intend to keep.

Past Janet stood by his bedside, armored in organization, wielding a folder labeled “Treatment Options – Version 7.3 (Revised)” like a shield against fate. Her voice carried the brittle hope of someone trying to bargain with death using spreadsheets as currency.

“If we adjust the schedule,” Past Janet was saying to a room that had already made other plans, “we can fit in both treatments. I’ve created a spreadsheet—”

“Janet,” Philip interrupted, his voice soft as remembered lullabies. “Come here.”

But Past Janet was already at the whiteboard, writing names and times like incantations, as if the right combination of words might keep the world from turning.

“We need to optimize the care rotation,” she continued, each word another brick in a wall that wouldn’t save them.

“Jan,” he tried again. “Please.”

The scene stretched like a shadow at sunset, focusing with cruel clarity on Philip’s face. He watched his wife’s desperate organizing with eyes that held nothing but love—and beneath that, a deep understanding that she was doing the only thing she knew how to do: trying to create order in a universe that was about to take away the one thing that had made its chaos bearable.

The memory began to fade as old photographs do, but before it dissolved completely, Janet saw one last thing: Philip, mouthing words she hadn’t seen then, words that tasted of forgiveness and autumn leaves: “I understand, my love. I always understood.”

The hospital room melted away like morning mist, leaving Janet in her kitchen where even the appliances held their breath. Her carefully ordered world looked different now, as if all her precise measurements had been elaborate equations trying to solve for the one variable that refused to be contained: loss.

“Congratulations,” Nimbus said softly, its voice carrying the weight of a tech support angel who had seen too many souls rebooted. Its light shifted slightly, catching Janet’s gaze like a nudge. “You’ve achieved what our engineers call a ‘Breakthrough Classic.’ Remember, balance begins with the smallest adjustments. Like that first moment—you and I, working together to find just the right place for me on your counter. Small steps lead to great journeys.”

Janet stood in her kitchen, watching the last pieces of her orderly universe rearrange themselves into something new and terrible and true. The measuring spoons hung like question marks on their hooks, and somewhere in the distance, a digital assistant was probably offering someone else a discount on their own personal apocalypse.

“He knew,” she whispered, and the words hung in the air like stars. “All this time, he knew.”

“Of course he did,” Nimbus replied, its voice carrying echoes of every oracle who’d ever traded in truths too large for temples. “Love predates all our careful categorizations. It was drawing hearts in the margins of the universe long before we thought to number its pages.”

There are three kinds of darkness in this world. There is ordinary darkness that comes with nightfall, the kind that streetlights and smartphones have largely banished from modern life. There is metaphorical darkness that poets write about, that lives in hearts and souls and the spaces between stars. And then there is the darkness that exists only in kitchens at midnight when the world has gone quiet enough to hear grief whispering your name.

Janet sat in this third kind of darkness now, in this space between breaking and healing. It wrapped around her shoulders like a shawl knitted from shadows and remembered laughter, while somewhere in the garage, Box E-17 hummed with the kind of silence that comes just before prophecies are fulfilled.

“There are messages in bottles,” Nimbus said softly, its voice carrying none of its usual digital cheer, “and then there are messages in boxes. Rachel asked me to show you what’s in E-17.” The AI paused, the way ancient oracles might have paused before revealing the part of the prophecy they knew would hurt. “Philip left something there, in that space between ‘now’ and ‘later’ where you file all your almost-memories.”

“If this is another emotional ambush–” Janet began, her fingers tracing the edge of the kitchen counter where time had worn away at its perfect right angles.

“No ambush,” Nimbus replied, with what might have been compassion if silicon dreams could hold such things. “Just truth. Sometimes they feel the same.”

The kitchen wall flickered to life, not with the harsh glare of modern displays, but with the soft luminescence of memory made manifest. Memory, that trickster god of the digital age, that keeper of moments we thought we’d filed away for good. The image spread like watercolor on wet paper, bleeding past the carefully drawn lines of Janet’s world: Philip, sitting at their kitchen table that last February midnight when the world was already beginning to fold its edges into endings.

He wore that ridiculous apron she’d given him – “Kiss the Cook (But First, Sanitize)” – the kind of joke that feels like prophecy in retrospect, a tiny banner declaring her eternal battle between love and order. The timestamp in the corner read 2:47 AM, a witch’s hour when truth tends to slip past our better defenses.

The Philip in the recording smiled the way people smile when they’re looking across time itself, across all the careful categories and labeled boxes, straight into the heart that’s trying to hide. “Hey there, Jan,” he said, and just those words – so ordinary, so infinite – made the carefully ordered universe of her kitchen tremble at its foundations.

At that moment, even her measuring spoons held their breath, waiting to see what happens when love finally picks the lock on a perfectly organized heart.

“Our daughter convinced the universe to let a little chaos slip through your defenses. I always knew Rachel had that power. She gets it from you, you know – that ability to make even disorder follow proper protocols.”

His laugh was soft, the kind that belongs to midnight conversations and other sacred hours when truth sneaks past our careful guards. “I can see you now, wondering how to categorize this moment. ‘Final Messages from the Almost-Dead,’ maybe? Sub-folder: ‘Things That Refuse to Be Organized,’ cross-referenced with ‘Proof That Philip Never Did Learn to Follow Proper Documentation Procedures.’”

His image leaned forward through the digital veil, and there was Philip – not Memory’s ghost or Death’s ledger entry, but her Philip, with that smile that had always made the universe’s sharp edges soften into possibility. The timestamp blinked in the corner like a star trying to remember its own name.

“Jan,” he said, and the kitchen held its breath, all its carefully measured spaces leaning in to listen. “You’ve spent your whole life teaching chaos how to behave, cataloging stardust, and filing infinity into properly labeled boxes. And somewhere along the way, you started thinking you were too much – too precise, too thorough, too... you. But that’s like saying time is too endless or love is too deep.”

He coughed then – the kind of cough that had become a familiar character in their story, making its entrances with the terrible timing of a Greek chorus, announcing tragedies everyone already knew were coming. Cancer had been collecting its payments in small installments of breath, but his eyes still held that same light that had once made her alphabetize their first kiss into categories of wonder.

“You could never be too much, my curator of everyday magic. Not when you’re measuring morning light in teaspoons of joy, not when you’re indexing the exact frequency of heartbeats. The world needs its collectors of scattered moments, its mathematicians of love.” The kitchen trembled with truth, its measuring spoons chiming softly like temple bells, while somewhere in the distance, Time itself paused to make a careful note in its eternal ledger: Here is where she finally understood.

“You were always enough,” Philip whispered, his words rippling through layers of digital memory like stones dropped in the well of forever. “Even when you’re teaching infinity to follow proper documentation procedures. Maybe especially then.”

Something broke inside her—like a dam giving way to waters it had held back for centuries. Janet’s carefully constructed world of order and control didn’t so much shatter as dissolve, like sugar in rain, like categories in chaos, like all the perfectly labeled boxes she’d tried to fit her heart into.

She cried. Not the neat, scheduled grief of memorial services and sympathy cards filed by sender. This was primordial grief, the kind that existed before humans invented calendars to measure loss. It came with snot and hiccups and sounds that would make small animals seek shelter, the kind of crying that isn’t pretty or organized or indexed for future reference.

Her tears fell on the kitchen floor, making small dark circles on the tiles she’d scrubbed to perfection just that morning. Each drop was a universe of regret: for the cakes unmade, for the moments unshared, for all the times she’d chosen order over presence. They spread like tiny galaxies across her perfect floor, each one a monument to the fact that some stains are meant to stay.

On the wall, Philip’s image smiled one last time, as if he could see her across the years, across the void, across all the space she’d put between herself and her own heart. “I love you,” he said simply. “Even the parts of you that need to sort the spice rack by emotional resonance. Especially those parts. They’re what make you Janet.”

The spaces between digital and analog grief filled with something ancient and new – the kind of magic that exists in the overlap between perfectly measured teaspoons and perfectly imperfect love. Even the AI assistants, those careful catalogers of human behavior, recognized that some things cannot be indexed, only felt.

“There,” Nimbus said softly. “Was that so hard?”

“Yes,” Janet managed between sobs. “Yes, it absolutely was.”

The next morning, Janet sat at her kitchen table, where the air felt lighter somehow, as though her home itself had exhaled a breath it hadn’t realized it was holding. Across the wall, her honeymoon memories unfurled like a film reel with a mind of its own. This wasn’t the tidy highlight reel she had curated in her head, scrubbed clean of imperfections, and edited to Instagram standards. No, these were the real moments—the ones with frayed edges and the kind of warmth that lives only in the imperfect.

Philip appeared first, arguing animatedly with a map in Venice, gesturing with a half-eaten gelato as though he might strong-arm the canals themselves into compliance. “It’s moved,” he insisted, his voice full of the conviction only a man who’d never learned to read a map properly could muster. The memory jumped forward: Janet, clutching her sides as laughter spilled out of her in great, unladylike snorts. Red wine sprayed from her nose as their hotel’s rebellious bidet staged a spectacular revolt, later immortalized on local news as “The Great Waterworks Incident.”

Nimbus’s voice, soft and warm, interrupted her thoughts, the tone of a teacher offering praise after a difficult lesson. “You’ve worked hard, Janet,” it said, glowing faintly as if to match the memories. “Regret weighs heavy, but reflection lightens the load. Consider this… a reward. The pieces of joy you thought you’d misplaced.”

The memories continued, more vibrant now as if the act of remembering had polished them. Philip getting hopelessly lost in Rome while “following his intuition,” which, as they later discovered, was a nose for freshly baked bread. Her carefully color-coded itinerary abandoned in favor of a street musician’s melody, the kind of detour that no planner could ever predict. Janet let herself sink into the memories, her lips curving into a smile that felt foreign but welcome as if her face were remembering how to wear it after years of disuse. “A reward,” she murmured, almost to herself, “or a reminder?”

Nimbus pulsed gently. “Sometimes, they’re the same thing.”

“That’s enough,” she said, smiling through tears that had finally learned to fall without first submitting a formal request through proper channels.

The TV awakened like an oracle who had learned its prophecies from marketing firms. “CONGRATULATIONS!” it announced, in the precise tone that ancient gods might have used when offering mortals particularly clever bargains. Digital confetti rained down, each pixel containing a perfectly optimized fragment of joy.

“You’ve completed Regret Processing Level One!” Words flashed in the color of discounted enlightenment, followed by smaller text that whispered of Premium+ subscriptions and emotional upgrades as if even memories now came with in-app purchases.

Janet gave the screen her teacher’s glare – the one that had survived three decades of budget cuts and at least one student who’d claimed his homework had achieved sentience and moved to Seattle. “After all this,” she said, “you’re just a common salesman, aren’t you?”

“I prefer to think of myself as a merchant of overlooked truths,” Nimbus replied, with the kind of warmth that crossroad demons reserve for particularly promising clients.“Besides, you needed me. Rather like how a sealed box in the garage needs someone to finally open it—not for what’s inside, but for the weight that lifts when it’s no longer waiting.”

Nimbus Companion™, on being called a salesman

“Thank you,” she murmured. Her voice wavered between bitterness and something gentler, perhaps gratitude. “Even if it was all to sell me... well, me.”

She stood and walked to her door – each step an unsigned declaration of independence from the algorithms that had become our modern seers. For the first time since Philip’s passing (three years, ten months, two days, and approximately fourteen hours – but Time had always been an unreliable narrator), she opened it without consulting her usual digital auguries.

“Don’t forget to rate your breakthrough!” Nimbus called after her, its voice trailing wisps of algorithmic mist through the doorway. “Five stars unlocks our Enlightenment Express path to digital nirvana! Though of course,” it added, its edges rippling with what might have been irony, or perhaps just a passing breeze of corporate compliance, “we would never dream of trading transcendence for testimonials.”

Janet — no, Ms. Halprin — paused at the threshold. Then she smiled and shook her head, tasting the syllables of her own name like a familiar recipe whose ingredients she’d forgotten. “Janet,” she said softly to herself, and for the first time in decades, it didn’t feel like surrender. She thought of Philip, who had loved both her precision and her chaos, who had called her “Jan” in moments of tenderness and “Ms. Halprin” in moments of play. She thought of Rachel, who had never quite mastered calling her “Mother” with the proper gravity (always “Mom” or “Mommy” or, in moments of teenage defiance, “Janet”). And she thought of herself, all her careful walls of honorifics and proper nouns, her fortress built of Ms. and Mrs. and the precisely measured distance between familiarity and respect.

Behind her, Nimbus’s voice floated out one last time: “Goodbye, Ms. Halprin.” And in that moment, Janet understood that names, like memories, like love itself, could exist in multiple states at once. She could be Ms. Halprin in her classroom, Janet in her kitchen, Jan in her heart, and all of them would be equally, perfectly true.

“Goodbye,” she replied and smiled. “And... you can call me Janet.”

Ms. Halprin stepped outside, leaving her house to its devices – literally. Inside, the “smart” appliances exchanged knowing glances, while Nimbus beeped a satisfied status update: “APPROVED. Processing payment for JANET: emotional growth surcharge.”

And somewhere on the cosmic balance sheet of the universe, a small box labeled Nothing was quietly crossed out and replaced with Something Very Important Indeed.

THE FILES · A WORKBOOK · CORRESPONDENCE COURSE NO. 1

How to Be a Secret Government Plant (Any Decade)

By Heather Chappell & Edwin Galloway

Seven lessons · Four unredacted files · One bingo card · One certificate

✎ Pencil only. Pen is permanent, and so is the paperwork.

Orientation: Welcome, Outsider

You want to run the country from the outside. Wonderful. Every great outsider in history had the same dream, and every one of them started by getting as far inside as the building allowed.

This workbook is satire. Every case is drawn from public reporting. Nobody in it is accused of being a government plant or of breaking any law.

One more thing before you start. Nobody in the history of this method has ever needed to be secretly planted. That’s the good news. You don’t need a handler in a trench coat. You need a government program, a good sense of timing and a straight face.

The government doesn’t plant outsiders. It just keeps watering them.

Sharpen your pencil.

Lesson 1: Get Rich From the Government (Quietly)

The first rule of the outsider: your money came from the inside. Not all of it. Just the part that mattered, at the moment it mattered. Study these hypothetical success stories.

Case 01 · late 1700s — The General in a Hurry

Leaves his army overseas. Comes home to a country that’s broke and nervous. The men who fund his takeover sell supplies to the army. Ten weeks after he takes over, the new government opens a national bank. Guess whose name is first on the list of shareholders. You’ll never guess. It’s his.

Case 02 · 1860s — The Railroad Man

Congress wants a railroad across the continent. It offers land and government bonds for every mile of track. Our hypothetical hero discovers a deep personal passion for track. Some of his track goes places. Some of it mainly goes on the invoice.

Case 03 · 1960s — The Computer Salesman

Congress creates a giant new health program for the elderly and the poor. Millions of claims need processing. Our hero happens to own a company that processes claims. Within a few years a large share of his sales comes from the program, and a business magazine calls him the fastest-rising rich man in his state.

Case 04 · 1970s — The Recluse With a Boat

A famously private billionaire lends his name to a ship that is supposedly mining the ocean floor for nodules. It is actually fetching a sunken Soviet submarine for the CIA. The nodule industry is still waiting.

Case 05 · 2000s — The Rocket Man

His rockets have blown up three times. He is almost out of money. The fourth one works, and a few months later the space agency needs a truck to haul cargo to orbit. He has a truck. The contract is worth more than a billion dollars. His car company, meanwhile, takes a federal loan and later sells “regulatory credits,” which other carmakers must buy because government rules say so. A national newspaper eventually adds it all up to tens of billions in public money, arriving “often at critical moments.” Critical moments are when money is most useful. That’s just finance.

Case 06 · 2000s — The Data Man

Private investors pass on his surveillance-software company. Then a spy agency’s venture fund writes an early check, and the spy agency becomes one of its first customers. Twenty years later, the government is its biggest customer and the Army has signed a deal worth up to ten billion dollars. Private enterprise at its finest.

Lesson 2: Choose Your Archetype

There are four jobs. Circle yours.

Read this as text

A. The Sword

Famous. Armed. Hired by insiders who believe they can put him back in the case.

The insiders want a takeover but can’t do it themselves, because everybody knows them. They need a famous face. Their first choice dies. Their second says no. Their third walks off a boat.

On the big day, the Sword walks into a room he wasn’t invited to and is nearly arrested. A younger sibling has to save him. About five years later he is wearing a crown he placed on his own head.

  • Strengths: Uniform. Name recognition. Confidence in rooms that did not ask for it.
  • Weakness: Needs rescuing by a sibling.
  • Your sponsors’ mistake: Thinking the case had a lock.

B. The Man With the Charts

Rich. Earnest. Right about the deficit. Knows the White House switchboard by voice.

He calls the White House so often that it assigns a staffer just to him. He funds a secret mission for a colonel whose name will later be very famous. A giant corporation pays him hundreds of millions to leave its board and fines him if he criticizes it in public. Then he runs for president as the man nobody can buy.

  • Strengths: Charts. Sincerity. Folksy sound effects.
  • Weakness: Calls the White House for eighteen years straight. Year eighteen: voicemail.

C. The Contractor Who Audits His Customer

Wants to shrink the government. Is one of its larger vendors. Gets hired to do the shrinking.

He spends a quarter of a billion dollars helping a candidate win. The candidate gives him a job cutting federal spending. He has a badge and an office. His companies have contracts. Everyone agrees this is fine. Four months later he leaves. A few weeks after that, he and his friend fight in public, and he announces a brand-new political party. By the next midterms he is funding the old one again.

  • Strengths: Rockets. Owns the platform he complains on, and the one he launches from.
  • Weakness: Is also, technically, on the list of things being audited.

D. The One Behind the Curtain

Never runs. Never needs to. Picks the sword.

In one century, it’s the bankers who sit quietly in the meetings, wait to see if the takeover works, and walk away with a national bank. In another, it’s a venture capitalist who hires a young memoirist, funds his first campaign, and watches him become vice president. The curtain changes fabric. It never changes function.

He once wrote that competition is for losers. We’ve dedicated this whole issue to losers. We’ll let him work out which side of the page he’s on.

  • Strengths: Patience. Money. An eye for swords.
  • Weakness: Everything’s on paper. Nobody reads the paper.

Lesson 6: The Falling-Out Flowchart

Every outsider’s friendship with a president ends here. Trace your path.

START: You get the president’s personal number.

Does he stop taking your calls? YES → You are having The Voicemail. Classic. Run against him in four to five years. NO: Post something. Check again tomorrow.

Do you own a platform to complain on? YES → You are having The Public Breakup. Announce a new party the day after the Fourth of July. NO: Buy one. See above.

Do you start a third party?

  • Yes, and I ran: Nineteen percent of the vote. Zero electoral votes. Nobody could buy you, and it showed.
  • Yes, and then I went back: This is called The Round Trip. Your PAC is funding the old party by the midterms. Nobody mentions it. Neither do you.
  • I never ran. I picked the guy: You are The Curtain. You win. You were never on this flowchart.
