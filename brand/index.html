Start here

SIDE EYE Brand Standards · Version 1.0 draft · November 24, 2026 · Owner: Heather

This is the one place for how SIDE EYE looks, sounds and gets made. It covers the magazine, the YouTube show, anything public, and the tools the team uses inside the company. If you make anything with the SIDE EYE name on it, this is your rulebook.

Who this is for

  • Editors, writers and fact-checkers working on the magazine or scripts.
  • Video editors, animators, designers and producers working on the show, thumbnails or print layouts.
  • Content managers and vendors posting, packaging or building anything public.
  • AI agents (Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini and others) helping any of the people above.

How to use it

  1. Read Brand core first. It takes five minutes and explains what SIDE EYE is for.
  2. Then read the page for your job: Visual identity, Voice and writing, The magazine, The YouTube show or Internal tools.
  3. Before you hand anything in, run the checklist for your role in Working with us.
  4. If you use an AI, load the AI file (see AI-ready files) before you start.

The golden rules

If you remember nothing else:

  1. Bold claims, honest proof. Say the surprising thing, then show exactly how sure we are. Never invent a quote, a fact or a source.
  2. Curious, never cruel. Punch up at the powerful and at ourselves. Never at victims, viewers or anyone's looks.
  3. Plain words. About an 8th-grade reading level. Short sentences. Explain every term once.
  4. It looks printed by hand. Neon ink on cream paper, cut and pasted. Never glossy, never "rendered on a screen."
  5. Pink is the signal. Side Eye Pink (#FF48B0) marks the thing to look at.
  6. Heather has the final yes. Nothing ships, posts or prints without her sign-off.

Where the official files live

  • Web: the private SIDE EYE standards page on Heather's site (address given to you directly; it is not linked or searchable).
  • Google Drive: Side Eye > Branding > Brand Standards Hub. Google Docs for people, .md files for AI.
  • If the web page and Drive ever disagree, the newest dated version wins, and tell Heather.

How this guide changes

Only Heather approves a change. Every change gets a new version number and a line in the Decision log. Older guides in Drive stay where they are for history, but this hub replaces them. If an older file disagrees with this one, this one wins.

Section 01

Brand core

What SIDE EYE is

SIDE EYE is a history magazine and a YouTube show made by two hosts, Heather and Edwin. It takes the stories everybody knows and finds the parts that fell out.

  • Tagline: History, allegedly.
  • The pitch: You were right to suspect the story you were told was wrong. The good news is the truth is more interesting.
  • What the name means: the look you give someone who's feeding you a line. Curious, skeptical, a little amused. Never sneering.

The promise

Every issue and every episode gives you one surprising, true idea about history, tells you exactly how sure we are, and makes you laugh on the way. We test the conspiracy. We don't assume it.

Who it's for

Curious adults, mostly millennials, who liked history class less than they like a good story. Think a smart friend at a party. (Internal shorthand only, never in public copy: part Mental Floss, part Spy magazine.) They are busy, skeptical of being sold to, and they read on their phones. Write at about an 8th-grade reading level, so a smart 13-year-old could follow it and a history professor couldn't fault it.

The two hosts

  • Heather tells the story like it's happening right now. She carries the personal and emotional stories, stretches toward the bold theory, and lands the heart of it.
  • Edwin follows the money and the record. He covers war, politics and the global machine, says plainly what can and can't be proven, and makes it funny by refusing to oversell.
  • Together they are curious amateurs with library cards. Neither claims to be an expert, a journalist, a professor or an economist. They enjoy each other, rib each other gently, and never make the other look stupid.
  • On video, hosts use first names only.

Things we never do

  • Invent quotes, facts, sources or personal details about the hosts.
  • Take any story further than the facts support. No subject is off limits for research; the standard is honest, fact-supported storytelling.

  • Put Heather's or Edwin's personal contact details, home location or last names on screen.

Section 02

Visual identity

The one idea: every SIDE EYE image looks like it was printed, pasted or carved by hand. Think 1990s art zines, neon silkscreen ink, letterpress show posters and carved-block prints, on warm cream paper. Never glossy, glowing or "rendered on a screen."

Source of truth for print art: Design System v3, Neon Zine Print (Sep 29, 2026), with the grid and page system kept from Design System v2 and the print specs in Addendum A.

Logo

  • The lockup: a sideways eye (cream outline, Side Eye Pink iris glancing right) next to the wordmark SIDE EYE in tall condensed capitals.
  • Default: cream logo on Toner Black. On cream paper, use the black version with the pink iris.
  • Tagline lockup: "HISTORY, ALLEGEDLY." in small mono capitals with wide letter spacing, under the masthead.
  • The flat eye logo is used alone on end screens and small spaces (app icons, avatars, corners).
  • Clear space: at least one iris width on every side, and the height of the eye when there is room.
  • Never: stretch it, recolor the iris anything but pink (one-ink files make it the same single color as the rest), add shadows or glows, outline the letters, put it on a busy photo without a solid field behind it, or rebuild the wordmark in another font.
  • Motion: only animations Heather has seen and approved. The approved base is the glancing eye from heatherchappell.com (see Logo animation in the Logo section).
  • Logo files and full rules: see the Logo section and its downloads. The vector pack (now v1.2) was redrawn from the channel banner.

AI-generated video

Generated footage illustrates an idea. It never stands in for real footage of a real person.

  • Use it for scenes, objects and moments: a newspaper being read, a letter being written, a fire in a fireplace, trees being cleared.
  • Never show a real person, living or historical, acting on screen in generated footage that a viewer could take for real film of them.
  • Fine: hands, objects, settings, silhouettes and clearly stylized art. Generated stills and stylized likenesses of historical figures stay allowed.
  • The pause test: if a viewer who paused the frame could believe it's real archival or news footage of that person, restyle it or cut it.

Image rights (every product)

  • Use only our own images, public-domain images, free-for-commercial-use images, or generated art. No paid licenses.
  • Every historical image needs a confirmed public-domain or free-license line before it's used.
  • No readable brand logos or personal information in found material.
  • Credit lines: generated art "Illustration generated with Midjourney and edited by SIDE EYE." Composites: "Figure from [source], cut out and altered by SIDE EYE; illustration generated with Midjourney and edited by SIDE EYE." Historical art: artist, title, date, holding institution. The magazine carries one Midjourney credit at the front.
  • Influences (zines, show posters, print studios) are credited in the colophon only, never named in prompts, captions or marketing.

Section 03

Voice and writing

Bold claims. Honest proof. We sound like a smart friend who read the footnotes and can't wait to tell you.

The basics

  • Reading level: about 8th grade. Short sentences (10 to 15 words is the sweet spot). Active voice. Concrete nouns.
  • Plain words: explain every term once, in kitchen-table terms ("a bond is an IOU").
  • Job tags: give every person a short job tag the first time they appear ("Walter Boyd, a London banker").
  • One modern frame per section: connect the past to something the reader knows, once, not in every paragraph.
  • Specifics beat adjectives: "ten thousand pounds" beats "a fortune."
  • American English, American dates (April 1, 1821), money in the original currency.
  • Use the right names: "the East India Company," never "the East India Trading Company."

Honest proof

We are only as bold as the evidence. Every claim carries how sure we are.

Research rating What it means Say it like this
Documented A primary source shows it State it plainly
Likely Good evidence, nothing against it "Probably," "the best evidence says"
Contested Late memoir, one source, or sources disagree "One account says," "he claimed"
Unsupported Claimed, no evidence found Only as a rumor, and say so
Refuted Documents contradict it Only to knock it down

Scripts shorten this to PROVEN, LIKELY, NOT PROVEN. These are writing tools for scripts and research. They never appear on screen as stamps or badges.

  • Never invent a quote. If a quote is a translation, say so. Keep the hedge with the claim.
  • Name who says what. One historian repeated by ten others is still one source.
  • Every magazine feature has a What We Can Prove / What We Can't box and an other side paragraph, stated fairly.
  • Kicker lines must be true on the evidence.

Headlines and titles

  • Magazine headlines: all caps, two or three beats, a bold arguable claim ("THE COUP WAS A JOB. NAPOLEON WAS THE HIRE.").
  • YouTube titles: 5 to 9 words, 45 characters or fewer, Title Case, one stakes word. A written title is never a question (the spoken title question in the cold open stays). Avoid years.
  • Teasers need a specific true "wow" fact, not a general idea.
  • The thesis comes first. Titles and thumbnails are written after the thesis is locked.

Words and phrases we never use

here's · let's · it gets stranger · keep an eye on · buckle up · but wait (there's more) · little did they know · the plot thickens · hold that · quietly · the real story · bless · delve · tapestry · it's worth noting · in a world where · said the quiet part out loud · for those who don't know · fun fact · plot twist · let that sink in · folks · fella (for Heather)

Also never:

  • "That's not X. That's Y." or any set-up and knock-down line. The one exception: Edwin may use this shape in his own comic voice.
  • Stacked rhetorical questions.
  • The "receipts" metaphor for evidence.
  • Hosts announcing their own research: no "And then we noticed something," "And then we hit the line that made us stop," "we hit a wall," "we found something," and no telling the viewer what they read.
  • "That's the version I got in school" and "things nobody reads for fun."
  • Hype words and exclamation-mark stacks.

Humor

  • Mockery goes up: the powerful, institutions, official stories, myths, ourselves.
  • Never at victims, the dead of a war or atrocity, ordinary people of the past, viewers, anyone's body, looks, accent, nationality, ethnicity or religion, or other creators by name.
  • No jokes within 15 seconds of a serious beat. Serious beats get full respect.
  • No profanity. SIDE EYE is an adult magazine. It can be fun, sexy, funny and grown up, in the best way, and sexual jokes are fine when they are appropriate. It is sold on Amazon, so it stays very far away from anything pornographic.

What's in every issue

  • Cover: one surreal idea, a cut-out hero with big eyes breaking the frame, masthead in a neon field, tagline "HISTORY, ALLEGEDLY." under the masthead, issue thesis line in serif italic under the issue title, 4 cover lines at most. The hero alternates sides by volume (Vol. 1 right, Vol. 2 left).
  • The money pages: two front spreads, the classifieds, the last spread and the back cover. Readable in under 15 seconds each, one point per infographic, no block over 120 words. Classifieds are 7 pt mono in two or three narrow columns, no images.
  • Corrections: printed every issue, in the same size type as the original error.
  • Features follow the fixed frame: headline and subhead, an opening that breaks the familiar story, the body, the twist, the What We Can Prove / What We Can't box, the other side, why the gap matters, and a sources line.
  • Recurring furniture: filing tabs (red, blue, gray), FILE NO. stamps, corner bars for recurring columns, coda marks, drop caps, the evidence rail.
  • Sources, the back-of-issue section, matched one to one with the fact-check log.
  • Colophon: one Midjourney credit, image credits, influence credits.

How a feature gets made

Research follows the Deep Research Process: frame a testable question, gather by research desks, fill gaps, get the books, write candidate claims, set proof standards before attacking them, red-team every claim, verify independently, then shape the thesis. Test the conspiracy, don't assume it.

Then: art direction (RELIEF, COLLAGE, WOOD TYPE, HYBRID or NEWSPRINT per image), layout, legal read last, KDP preflight, proof copy, listing. Understanding comes first, legal check comes last.

  • Every YouTube video links to the magazine in its description.

Section 05

The YouTube show

Channel: Side Eye Magazine (@SideEyeMag). Two hosts, Heather and Edwin. The model episode is the Emu War.

Formats

Format Length Notes
Long-form episode about 20 to 25 min The main show. Voiceover-first, with chosen on-camera moments
Series video varies Two-host videos in a numbered series
Case File 18 to 22 min Conspiracy investigation built from a magazine feature
Shorts 45 to 60 s Fun and visual: cutouts and paper dolls, light soap-opera tone, not document-heavy

Default episode structure

New episodes use the Reckless Ben shape (rb-episode-shape and rb-research-loop) with the 01b cold-open formula: no planted clue in the intro, then 8 to 12 discovery loops (hit a wall, do a surprising thing, got 'em, they don't want us to know, wall again). The older 01 template (25 minutes, 49 beats, a withheld object in the cold open) is replaced (Heather, Nov 24). Case Files built from a magazine feature still use side-eye-case-file-script.

How every long-form episode opens

  1. Heather sets up what we all think we know, in one long chunk: a thing in a place with a date, then the common belief.
  2. Edwin takes the second chunk with three or four concrete "Why would…?" questions that don't fit.
  3. Heather lands the title question on its own line.
  4. Then the standing intro:

HEATHER: I'm Heather. EDWIN: I'm Edwin. We make SIDE EYE, a magazine about history. HEATHER: Allegedly. EDWIN: We ask the questions everyone's thinking. HEATHER: But no one will give you the straight answer.

No branding, greeting or disclaimer before the cold open. No answer, twist or verdict in it. Model: the Napoleon cold open (EP02).

The disclaimer comes after the open, once:

We're not journalists. We're curious amateurs with library cards. When it's our opinion, we'll say so, and the sources are in the description so you can check them yourself.

Hosts on screen

  • Split: long alternating chunks, never ping-pong. Who goes first alternates from one episode to the next. Four or five gentle ribbing moments per episode.
  • The engine: Heather stretches toward the bold theory; Edwin says what can and can't be proven, then often adds a fact that's even wilder.
  • Never: hosts announcing what they read or found; stills or stand-in footage for host lines that weren't filmed; fake glitter or effects on Heather.
  • Framing: best take wins. Keep Heather framed wide by default.
  • Color grades: Heather uses the approved "Dim Room" grade for her living-room setup; Edwin uses the "Clean Creator" grade (see the grading skills).

Structure and pace

  • One story with a spine. Every tease, plant and question pays off before the end.
  • No context block over 90 seconds. Something on screen changes every 8 to 10 seconds.
  • No chapter breaks or title cards mid-episode. YouTube description chapters are fine and their titles should be funny.
  • Always end on a true twist.
  • Serious beats get full animation, no jokes, no gag elements.
  • About 130 to 145 spoken words a minute.

Packaging

  • Titles: 5 to 9 words, 45 characters or fewer, Title Case, one stakes word. Never a question in writing; the spoken title question in the cold open stays.
  • Thumbnails: Heather and Edwin always, three elements at most, words that add to the title. Make six to choose from, then test the best two.
  • Description: magazine link, sources link (short link to the sources doc), funny chapter titles.
  • Captions ship as a separate .srt file.
  • End screen: flat eye logo on the Magazine-forward template by default. The other templates are only for cases Heather names.
  • Logo motion: only the approved glancing-eye animation, or the logo cutting, fading, moving or scaling evenly. New motion goes to Heather first.
  • Upload master: 1440p.

Music

The original SIDE EYE theme, or stock music that is free for commercial use and isn't claimed when the video is uploaded to YouTube. Check the upload for claims before it goes public, and swap any claimed track. ("Investigative" by Stereo_Color was removed after a claim; don't reuse it.)

Internal tools

Rules for anything the team uses but the audience never sees: the production app, intranet pages, trackers, dashboards and this hub. Internal tools should look like SIDE EYE, but calmer. They are workbenches, not magazine spreads.

The basics

  • Plain words on buttons and labels. Say what happens: "Approve script", not "Proceed".

Accessibility

  • Tap targets are at least 44 px.
  • Support dark mode by following the device setting.

  • Every gate records who approved it and when.

Privacy

Internal tools that live on the web are private: not linked from public pages, marked noindex, and blocked in robots.txt. No personal phone numbers, emails or home locations on any screen.

For vendors, editors and AI agents

Read this first if you're new to SIDE EYE, whether you're a person or an AI.

Checklists by role

Writers and script editors

  • Thesis is a clear claim in the first lines.
  • Sentences mostly 10 to 15 words, 8th grade reading level.
  • Every claim has a source and a proof level (in the script, never on screen).
  • No banned words or phrases. No "receipts" metaphor.
  • "That's not X. That's Y" only in Edwin's voice.
  • First names only for the hosts. Edwin never claims a profession.
  • Hosts never announce what they read or found.

Video editors

  • Cold open follows the formula and gives nothing away.
  • Standing intro and disclaimer used word for word.
  • No title cards or chapter breaks mid-episode. No proof stamps on screen.
  • Generated footage shows scenes, objects, hands or settings, never a real person acting on screen. It passes the pause test (no frame could pass for real film of that person). Period art never recolored.
  • "Opinion" tag in the corner when it's opinion.
  • Pop-ups 12 words or fewer. Something changes every 8 to 10 seconds.
  • Host grades applied (Dim Room for Heather, Clean Creator for Edwin). Best take wins. No effects on Heather.

Designers and art directors

  • Correct process chosen per page (RELIEF, COLLAGE, WOOD TYPE, HYBRID, NEWSPRINT).

  • Type at 7 pt or larger. No AI-generated lettering.

  • Every image has a credit and a cleared license.

Thumbnail and packaging

  • Heather and Edwin in the thumbnail, three elements at most.

  • Description links the magazine and the sources doc.
  • Six thumbnail options to choose from, then the best two tested.

  • Private pages marked noindex and blocked in robots.txt.

How the logo is built

All numbers are in logo units. One unit is one pixel of the master banner (2560 x 1440).

  • The eye is 360 units wide and 124.6 tall. Its outer shape is two circle arcs (radius 291.3) that meet in two sharp points. The outline is 18 units thick all the way round. Small-size eye: for 24 px and under there is a second drawing with a 30-unit outline. The outer shape, the sharp points, the upper lid line and the iris center are the same. The heavier outline makes the lower lid and the opening narrower. Never use it above 24 px.
  • The upper lid is a straight line 17 units above the middle. Everything above it is solid, which makes the heavy lid.
  • The iris is a pink circle 72 units across, set 78 units right of center. The lid line cuts its top, and its lower edge tucks behind the lower lid.
  • The wordmark is SIDE EYE in Anton, tracked +59/2048 em, cap height 201. Never retype it.
  • The tagline is HISTORY, ALLEGEDLY. in IBM Plex Mono SemiBold, cap height 36, letter pitch 37.2. It is the first part to break up when small.
  • The primary lockup: the eye's lowest point sits on the wordmark baseline. The eye is 0.62 of the cap height. The gap from the eye's point to the S is 61 units. The tagline starts flush with the S, 33 units below the baseline.
  • Stacked: the eye is centered over the wordmark, 61 units above the cap line, the same gap as in the horizontal lockup.

Clear space

X is the width of the pink iris in the file you are using (72 units).

## Voice and writing

**Bold claims. Honest proof.** We sound like a smart friend who read the footnotes and can't wait to tell you.

### The basics

- **Reading level:** about 8th grade. Short sentences (10 to 15 words is the sweet spot). Active voice. Concrete nouns.
- **Plain words:** explain every term once, in kitchen-table terms ("a bond is an IOU").
- **Job tags:** give every person a short job tag the first time they appear ("Walter Boyd, a London banker").
- **One modern frame per section:** connect the past to something the reader knows, once, not in every paragraph.
- **Specifics beat adjectives:** "ten thousand pounds" beats "a fortune."
- **American English,** American dates (April 1, 1821), money in the original currency.
- **Use the right names:** "the East India Company," never "the East India Trading Company."

### Honest proof

We are only as bold as the evidence. Every claim carries how sure we are.

| Research rating | What it means | Say it like this |
| :- | :- | :- |
| Documented | A primary source shows it | State it plainly |
| Likely | Good evidence, nothing against it | "Probably," "the best evidence says" |
| Contested | Late memoir, one source, or sources disagree | "One account says," "he claimed" |
| Unsupported | Claimed, no evidence found | Only as a rumor, and say so |
| Refuted | Documents contradict it | Only to knock it down |

Scripts shorten this to **PROVEN, LIKELY, NOT PROVEN**. These are writing tools for scripts and research. They never appear on screen as stamps or badges.

- Never invent a quote. If a quote is a translation, say so. Keep the hedge with the claim.
- Name who says what. One historian repeated by ten others is still one source.
- Every magazine feature has a **What We Can Prove / What We Can't** box and an **other side** paragraph, stated fairly.
- Kicker lines must be true on the evidence.

### Headlines and titles

- **Magazine headlines:** all caps, two or three beats, a bold arguable claim ("THE COUP WAS A JOB. NAPOLEON WAS THE HIRE.").
- **YouTube titles:** 5 to 9 words, 45 characters or fewer, Title Case, one stakes word. A written title is never a question (the spoken title question in the cold open stays). Avoid years.
- **Teasers** need a specific true "wow" fact, not a general idea.
- **The thesis comes first.** Titles and thumbnails are written after the thesis is locked.

### Words and phrases we never use

here's · let's · it gets stranger · keep an eye on · buckle up · but wait (there's more) · little did they know · the plot thickens · hold that · quietly · the real story · bless · delve · tapestry · it's worth noting · in a world where · said the quiet part out loud · for those who don't know · fun fact · plot twist · let that sink in · folks · fella (for Heather)

Also never:

- **"That's not X. That's Y."** or any set-up and knock-down line. The one exception: Edwin may use this shape in his own comic voice.
- Stacked rhetorical questions.
- **The "receipts" metaphor** for evidence.
- Hosts announcing their own research: no "And then we noticed something," "And then we hit the line that made us stop," "we hit a wall," "we found something," and no telling the viewer what they read.
- "That's the version I got in school" and "things nobody reads for fun."

## Logo

### How the logo is built

All numbers are in logo units. One unit is one pixel of the master banner (2560 x 1440).

- **The eye** is 360 units wide and 124.6 tall. Its outer shape is two circle arcs (radius 291.3) that meet in two sharp points. The outline is 18 units thick all the way round. **Small-size eye:** for 24 px and under there is a second drawing with a 30-unit outline. The outer shape, the sharp points, the upper lid line and the iris center are the same. The heavier outline makes the lower lid and the opening narrower. Never use it above 24 px.
- **The upper lid** is a straight line 17 units above the middle. Everything above it is solid, which makes the heavy lid.
- **The iris** is a pink circle 72 units across, set 78 units right of center. The lid line cuts its top, and its lower edge tucks behind the lower lid.
- **The wordmark** is SIDE EYE in Anton, tracked +59/2048 em, cap height 201. Never retype it.
- **The tagline** is HISTORY, ALLEGEDLY. in IBM Plex Mono SemiBold, cap height 36, letter pitch 37.2. It is the first part to break up when small.
- **The primary lockup:** the eye's lowest point sits on the wordmark baseline. The eye is 0.62 of the cap height. The gap from the eye's point to the S is 61 units. The tagline starts flush with the S, 33 units below the baseline.
- **Stacked:** the eye is centered over the wordmark, 61 units above the cap line, the same gap as in the horizontal lockup.

### Clear space

**X is the width of the pink iris** in the file you are using (72 units).

- Leave at least **1X** on every side of every layout. For the wordmark alone, use the cap height divided by 2.8.
- When there is room, as on covers, posters and end screens, leave the **height of the eye** (about 1.75X).
- Measure from the eye's points, the letter edges and the tagline's comma.
- Files named `on-black`, `on-cream` and `plate` already hold 1X inside their edges.
- Icons follow each platform's own safe zone, not X.
- **Don't** let text, other logos, rules or busy picture detail into the clear space.

# SIDE EYE skills (for Claude users)

Version 1.0 draft, 2026-11-24. Claude can load these skills. ChatGPT and Gemini can't, so this file is separate from the brand guide.

## Skills

A skill is a saved instruction set that a Claude agent loads to do one SIDE EYE job the house way. Other AIs, like ChatGPT or Gemini, can't load a skill, but you can paste a skill's rules into them as instructions.

### How the skills fit together

The sections below follow this order: research first, then print, then the video script, then the shoot, then the finish.

**(a) A YouTube episode, from research to upload**

1. Rank the best facts in the research: `top-research-surprises`
2. Lay out the story as one chain of causes: `cause-and-effect-story`
3. Pick the episode's shape, hook and ending: `rb-episode-shape`
4. Write the 8 to 12 discovery loops: `rb-research-loop`
5. Write the full two-host script: `side-eye-rb-two-host-script` (for a Case File made from a magazine feature, use `side-eye-case-file-script`; for the podcast version, use `heather-and-edwin-podcast-banter`)
6. Split the script between the hosts and add gentle ribbing: `side-eye-host-split-banter` (proposed, not installed yet)
7. Write or check every Edwin line: `side-eye-edwin-voice`
8. Set up the on-camera lines to read while filming: `teleprompter-notepad`
9. Color-correct the footage: `side-eye-grade-heather` and `side-eye-grade-edwin`
10. Make the final files and upload: `side-eye-final-output`

**(b) A magazine feature**

1. Rank the best facts in the research: `top-research-surprises`
2. Lay out the story as one chain of causes (optional): `cause-and-effect-story`

4. Plan the art and write the image prompts: `side-eye-art-direction`

### Fact ranker (`top-research-surprises`)
**In one line:** Scores every fact in the research for impact, surprise and story fit, then builds a checked top list.
- **Use it when:** The research is done and you need to choose which facts the story will be built on.
- **You give it:** Every research file for the topic: fact ledgers with fact IDs, rulings, claims scorecards (with their Must NOT lists) and conclusions. Optional: topic categories to sweep, a fact you want included, or outside material (another AI chat, an article) to check.
- **You get back:** A reply in chat with the top 13 to 15 facts in one table (new facts marked "new"), more facts grouped by category, a "things to stop saying" list of popular claims the research shows are wrong, and a note on where the full scored tables are saved. Each fact is scored 1 to 5 for impact, surprise and relevance, with a proof level.
- **Brand rules it enforces:**
  - Our own research beats outside sources, other AI chats and memory.
  - No fact comes from memory. Every new fact needs a cited source.
  - Famous facts score low on surprise, even when they matter.
  - Unproven facts go on a separate "tempting but unproven" list, never in the main ranking.
  - Each fact keeps its hedge, in plain 8th-grade words, with no opinion labels.
- **Don't use it for:** Writing the story, script or article. It only ranks and checks facts.
- **Area:** Both

### Story chain (`cause-and-effect-story`)
**In one line:** Tells the topic as one chain where each event causes the next, at an 8th-grade level.
- **Use it when:** You are planning or pitching an episode and want the whole spine on one page, before or beside the script.
- **You give it:** The episode title and current cold open, every shape or outline written for the topic (including options that weren't picked), the fact base with proof levels, and the main threads you want running through the whole story.
- **You get back:** In chat: the story (an opening, then sections headed "Because [cause], [effect].", then an ending), a side-by-side timeline table, and a short checks note on facts to verify before air.
- **Brand rules it enforces:**
  - Every "because" must be true. Missing links get added, and dates must run in order.
  - Proof levels stay as they are. A likely fact keeps its hedge in the same sentence, and no claim gets upgraded to fit the chain.
  - If a link is the hosts' own reading, like a person's motive, the checks note says so.
  - Real words are quoted exactly.
  - Short sentences, one short paragraph per section.
- **Don't use it for:** A finished script or article. It is a planning tool.
- **Area:** Both

### Feature writer (`conspiracy-feature-writer`)
**In one line:** Writes a finished, honestly sourced magazine feature from a completed research package, with cover options first.
- **Use it when:** The research and scorecard are done and you need the feature for SIDE EYE or for the Conspiracy Theories That Are Actually True Journal.
- **You give it:** The research summary and scorecard (with Must NOT claim, corrections, the other side and before-print checks), the sources doc with ratings, the raw research if there is any, and the section headings of the other features in the same issue.
- **You get back:** Five cover options (headline, subhead and teaser) when asked or when no angle is chosen. Then a feature of about 3,000 to 3,600 words, saved as a Google Doc in the topic's folder with a local copy, and a short reply: headline, shape, section headings, twist, word count, what the reviewer caught and the before-print checks left.
- **Brand rules it enforces:**
  - No scorecard with a Must NOT claim list means no writing. That list is binding on every sentence.
  - Each feature gets its own shape (a stack of bold claims, or a timeline). No stock section names, and nothing repeats from the previous feature.
  - A heading or kicker can only claim what the scorecard supports. Weaker claims become questions.
  - Every feature has a "What we can prove / What we can't" box, "The other side" and a sources line.
  - 8th-grade reading level, sentences of 10 to 15 words, a job tag on each person's first mention, no hype words.
  - An independent reviewer agent checks the draft before it goes out.
- **Don't use it for:** Doing the research, or writing the YouTube script.
- **Area:** Magazine
